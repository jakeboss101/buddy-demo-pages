How to Build a Campfire (and Put It Out Safely)

September 29, 2026 · 5 min read

A fire keeps you warm, dries wet clothes, boils water and lifts your spirits on a cold night. Every fire needs three things, called the fire triangle: heat, fuel and air. Take any one away and it goes out.

Before you start
  • Check for fire bans. Many parks ban fires in dry seasons.
  • Use an existing fire ring when there is one.
  • Clear 10 feet (3 m) around it, away from tents and low branches.
  • Keep a full bucket of water or a shovel and dirt close by.

Gather three sizes of wood

  • Tinder: things that light from a single spark: dry grass, birch bark, pine needles, cotton balls with petroleum jelly. Gather two big handfuls.
  • Kindling: dry sticks from match-thin up to thumb-thick. Gather an armload.
  • Fuel wood: wrist-thick to arm-thick dead, dry wood. If it snaps cleanly, it's dry enough.

Collect only dead and fallen wood. Never cut live trees.

Build it

  1. Tinder bundle. Place a loose ball of tinder in the center of the ring.
  2. Teepee. Lean thin kindling over it in a cone, leaving a gap on the side the wind comes from.
  3. Light it low. Light the tinder at the bottom, on the windward side. Blow gently at the base.
  4. Feed it slowly. Add thicker kindling as it catches, then fuel wood one piece at a time.
  5. Log cabin (optional). For a long-lasting fire, stack fuel wood around the teepee in a square.

Wet day tricks

  • Look for dry wood under fallen logs and on the lower dead branches of pine trees.
  • Split wet sticks; the inside is often dry.
  • Carry a backup: waterproof matches, a lighter and a ferro rod.

Put it out: drown, stir, feel

  1. Drown the fire with water until the hissing stops.
  2. Stir the ashes with a stick and add more water.
  3. Feel with the back of your hand, close but not touching. If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave.

Never leave a fire unattended, not even for a minute. If it's cool to the touch, you're good to go.
